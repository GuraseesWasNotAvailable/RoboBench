Methodology · datasets

How we grade a dataset

The policy benchmark fixes the data and varies the model. This one fixes everything except the data: same model, same recipe, same training run, same seeds, same evaluation scenes. A dataset's grade is the performance of a policy trained on it — because for demonstration data, that is the only definition of quality that isn't a guess.

1 · Why a dataset can only be graded by training on it

Data-quality tooling for images and text scores things you can see without a model: duplicates, corrupt files, label disagreement. A robot demonstration has no label to be wrong. Its quality is a property of the trajectory — whether the grasp was decisive, whether the path was direct, whether the episode shows the task being done or being fumbled — and none of that has a fixed relationship to downstream performance that you can assert in advance.

So the scoring function is the expensive one: train a fixed reference policy on the corpus and evaluate it. Everything cheap on this site — the six-property screen on the results page — is validated against that measurement, not substituted for it.

This is the whole reason the benchmark is defensible. Anyone can compute heuristics over a dataset. The claim that a given heuristic predicts what a trained policy will do requires a corpus of (dataset → trained policy → measured score) observations, and that corpus only exists if someone pays for the training runs. It is also why the grades improve as more datasets are graded.

2 · One model for every corpus

Every corpus is scored with GR00T N1.7-3B, fine-tuned with an identical recipe on a single NVIDIA L4 24 GB. No per-corpus hyperparameter search and no re-tuning.

One per-corpus choice is made, and it is the one worth disclosing. The corpora overfit at different rates — PH peaks at 500 steps and is 13 points worse by 1,000 — so the checkpoint scored is the best of the four on that corpus's learning curve. That selection happens on a separate seed set and the winner is re-scored on fresh seeds, which stops it leaking into the reported number, but it is still a per-corpus decision and calling the protocol "identical" without it would be false. MH's reported 24.20 comes from a 250-step checkpoint, a quarter of the nominal budget.

The bias this introduces is stated rather than hidden: a grade is measured through one model. A corpus that suits GR00T's action representation and fails another architecture would be scored generously here. Adding a second reference model is the top item on the results page's what's-next list for exactly this reason.

3 · Frames, not episodes

Corpora are matched on total frames — 9,666 / 9,698 / 9,750, within 0.9% — and never on episode count. This is the single most important design choice on this page.

Episode-matching silently confounds quality with volume. A careless operator produces longer episodes, so 100 careless episodes carry far more training signal than 100 clean ones. Matching episodes would let a bad corpus win on sheer quantity and would make "quality" unmeasurable. Matching frames means every corpus gets the same amount of gradient, and the only thing left varying is what those frames depict.

The same rule is applied to the curation experiment, where the two arms are frame-matched to 0.03%. An arm that wins because it was fed more data has not demonstrated anything about selection.

4 · How the score works

Binary success is too rare to rank on — the worst corpus here scores 0 successes in 100 attempts, and so does a random policy. Ranking on a metric where two very different things both score zero is not ranking. So the score is robosuite's staged physics progress, which reports how far into the task the policy actually got:

reach → 0–30  ·  grasp → jumps to 60  ·  lift height → 60–100

The stages come from simulator physics, not from a judge model or a human rater, so the number is a deterministic function of the logged rollout. Re-scoring against a future metric requires no GPU and no re-run.

Conditioned transitions, not just the total

The scalar ranks corpora; it does not say what to do about one. So each stage is also reported conditioned on the previous stage succeeding — grasp given reach, lift given grasp. That converts a score into a funnel and locates the constriction. On this task it showed that lifting never fails once an object is held, and that everything a policy cannot do is concentrated in closing the fingers.

The screen is validated, not assumed

The six cheap properties — success rate, gripper transitions, jerk, path efficiency, grasp timing, episode length — are computed on the raw corpus and then checked against what the trained policies actually did. Path efficiency, for instance, carries an odds ratio of 5.42 (2.49 – 11.81) for episode success. A property that failed this check would be dropped, however intuitive it sounded.

5 · Statistics and when we call it inconclusive

Means carry bootstrap 95% intervals. Rates carry Wilson intervals, which behave correctly near 0% and 100% where the normal approximation does not — and several cells here sit at exactly 0%. Where several behaviours are tested at once, p-values carry a Benjamini–Hochberg correction, because testing six hypotheses and reporting the best one is how noise gets published.

The curation A/B was specified in full before it ran — arms, checkpoint, step count, learning rate, seeds and sample size. The diagnostic analyses were exploratory, and are labelled as such: two early findings (spatial coverage, and an unnormalised gripper-oscillation metric) did not survive a larger sample or a length correction.

The rule we actually apply. The curation experiment — the forward test of the whole screen — moved every measure in the predicted direction and won 14 scenes to 6. Its 95% interval on the difference runs from −7.53 to +28.94 at n = 20, so it is reported as inconclusive on the results page and in the summary, in the same size type as the encouraging numbers. An interval that crosses zero is not a small effect; it is an unresolved question.

6 · The cross-dataset rule

A diagnosis produced from one corpus is not safe to act on. Grading three corpora at once makes a distinction that single-dataset analysis structurally cannot:

  • A bottleneck that tracks the data source — reaching, at 0.97 / 0.73 / 0.17 across PH / MH / MG — is a data problem. Better demonstrations fix it.
  • A bottleneck that sits in the same place regardless of source — grasping, at 0.17 and 0.14 for two corpora that are both 100% successful, where the difference is not resolvable at this sample size (+0.04, interval −0.18 to +0.23, p = 0.73) — is a candidate recipe problem, worth testing as one before a collection budget is spent. A failure to detect a difference is not proof of equivalence; what earns the reading here is that the recipe fix then worked.

On this task the second case was worth 5.9 points, recovered by changing one inference-time parameter and retraining nothing. Read from PH alone, the same evidence would have prescribed collecting more grasp demonstrations — an expensive answer to a question that was free.

This is why a dataset grade is reported alongside a verdict on whether data is the right lever at all. A grading service that always concludes "collect more data" is selling collection, not measurement.

7 · What we deliberately do not measure

  • Generalisation across tasks. One task, one object. Whether the acceptance thresholds hold for a task with a different dominant failure mode is untested, and it is the most likely thing to break.
  • Model-independence. One reference policy. A grade measured through GR00T N1.7 may not be the grade another architecture would assign.
  • Real hardware. Simulation only. Nothing here says how a corpus behaves on a physical robot.
  • Scale. Roughly 9,700 frames per corpus. Whether the ordering survives at 100× the data — where a noisy corpus may become tolerable — is unknown.
  • Provenance and licensing. We grade what a corpus teaches, not whether it was lawfully collected or carries consent for the people in it.

8 · Reproducibility

Evaluation uses paired seeds across arms, with determinism verified rather than assumed — arms see identical seeds — 100 held-out scenes for the corpus comparison, 20 paired scenes for the curation A/B — so a difference between arms cannot be a difference in scene difficulty. Roughly 1,340 rollouts are recorded across 39 evaluation records, per-episode and per-step.
